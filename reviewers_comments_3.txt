Answer: The reviewer bet that strict matching would contradict us. It does the opposite. He asked for the relative frequency of rotation-equivalent / non-rotation-in-set / out-of-set four-chord windows. Here they are: Lastfm 2.22% / 3.90% / 93.88%; Suno 9.44% / 7.95% / 82.61%; Udio 2.90% / 4.65% / 92.45%. The number that ends the argument: among windows built on an axis chord set, the share that are the literal loop (a rotation) is 54.3% for Suno, 36.3% for Lastfm, 38.5% for Udio. Suno is not "using the same four common chords." It plays the loop, in order, ~1.5x more than humans. We then ran his preferred strict rotation-only definition (12 patterns, zero reordering). Suno's lead over humans does not shrink. It grows at every threshold: >=10% 2.51x -> 4.09x; >=25% 3.30x -> 4.90x; >=50% 4.54x -> 7.34x. 

He wrote: "I'm sure you'll find the numbers defend the Richards side and contradict the current paper approach." They defend Richards and they strengthen our result. That is the only way to compare harmonic function across songs in different keys. The same pipeline runs on all three corpora, so any key-estimation error hits all three equally and cannot create the gaps we report. Single global-key estimation is listed as a limitation in Section 6.1.

Specifically, my concern lies in the data collection process for the Udio and Suno tracks. While this issue was raised during the previous review cycle by another reviewer and myself, the explanation provided in the authors' response does not fully resolve this methodological vulnerability.In the original manuscript, the authors stated that Suno songs were sourced from the "New Songs" playlist, whereas Udio songs were selected based on popularity (prioritizing the most plays within a 24-hour window). In the revised version, the detail regarding Suno remains, but the reference to Udio's popularity-based selection has been removed. The rationale for this omission is not directly addressed in the authors' response; instead, they note that Udio tracks were scraped from the front page and state that because platform recommendation algorithms are undisclosed, they cannot be commented upon. I find this unexplained shift in the reported data collection process concerning. Furthermore, the authors assert that they "did not follow any selection criteria in forming our dataset."I must respectfully challenge the confidence placed in this assumption. Even if the data collection relied strictly on scraping the "New Songs" playlist for Suno and the front page for Udio, the opaque nature of these platforms makes it impossible to guarantee that the underlying curation mechanisms are equivalent. If one dataset (Suno) is driven purely by recency, while the other (Udio) is curated by an algorithm that factors in user engagement or popularity, the resulting datasets are inherently shaped by two divergent filtering criteria.This distinction is highly critical, as a popularity-based filter acts as a proxy for human preference. Consequently, it introduces a significant confounding variable into the platform comparisons. The manuscript concludes that Suno generates less original content, whereas Udio produces more diverse and original music (e.g., lines 866–871). However, if Udio's dataset is inadvertently filtered by popularity, we must question whether Udio's models are inherently generating more original music, or if human listeners actively favor and upvote the most compelling tracks from the platform's broader output. In other words, it is unclear whether the observed diversity stems from the model's baseline capabilities, or from a human-driven selection bias that systematically surfaces the most creative songs, or, alternatively, those that align most closely with their pre-existing musical preferences.While this potential bias does not invalidate the automated analysis methodology itself, it does introduce a layer of uncertainty into any comparative conclusions drawn between Suno and Udio. I strongly advise the authors to explicitly address this selection bias as a limitation in the manuscript. Specifically, the comparative conclusions should be carefully nuanced to acknowledge that the observed differences could stem either from the underlying generative models themselves or from the differing selection biases inherent to the platforms' curation algorithms. This could also present a compelling avenue for future work: comparing randomly selected tracks versus popularity-filtered tracks generated by the same model to determine if human curation systematically favors specific structural or harmonic patterns.Although this remains a substantive concern, I believe the authors can adequately address it through careful textual revisions without necessitating a subsequent round of peer review. My assessment of the paper's originality is unchanged from the first round. The study makes a timely and meaningful contribution by applying large-scale MIR techniques to questions that are explicitly musicological, concerning harmonic practice, formal organisation, and stylistic convergence in AI-generated popular music. The scale of the corpus (60,014 tracks), the comparative framing between two commercially deployed generative platforms and a human-composed reference corpus, and the openly released dataset all represent genuine contributions to an emerging area of scholarship that sits between MIR and musicology.The revision strengthens the originality in two respects. First, the new Axis of Awesome analysis (Section 4.5) extends the harmonic comparison from trigrams to four-chord windows, providing a more direct connection to the musicological literature on canonical pop progressions. Second, the adoption of semantic section labels (verse, chorus, bridge, etc.) through the All-In-One model means that the BPE analysis now operates on musically interpretable tokens, a substantial improvement over the cluster-number approach of the original submission. Together, these changes sharpen the musicological significance of the findings.The broad conclusions remain confirmatory. The observation that Suno disproportionately reproduces late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century pop harmonic schemas, and that loop-based formal structures dominate its output, will not surprise musicologists who have worked with these platforms. This is not a criticism so much as an acknowledgement that the paper's primary contribution lies in providing empirical substantiation for what has, until now, been anecdotal knowledge.

3. Methodology:
Sufficient methodology
Comments/Explanation:
The revision represents a significant methodological improvement. The replacement of the custom ACE pipeline with the consonance-aware model of Poltronieri et al. (2025), and the replacement of Laplacian segmentation with the All-In-One model (Kim and Nam, 2023), address the principal concerns raised in the first round. Both models are peer-reviewed, publicly available, and applied without modification, which removes the validation burden that weighed on the original submission.Two observations from a music-analytical perspective.First, the Axis of Awesome analysis (Section 4.5) treats all 24 orderings of each four-chord set as valid match targets, yielding 72 distinct patterns. This decision merits a sentence of justification. Musicologically, chord order is consequential; I–V–vi–IV, IV–I–V–vi, and I–IV–V–vi may draw on the same pitch material, but they do not carry the same syntactic or formal implications. Richards (2017), whom the authors cite, discusses precisely this kind of tonal ambiguity arising from reordering. As defined here, the reported axis rates measure membership in an axis-family harmonic vocabulary more than recurrence of a single functional progression. The comparative findings are still useful, but the paper should note this distinction explicitly.Second, the revised BPE analysis of semantic section labels is a substantial improvement over the original submission. The All-In-One model provides musically interpretable labels, and the paper now states the key limitation clearly: these labels are acoustically assigned, so a verse–chorus alternation in AI output may reflect static loop contrast rather than functional song form (Section 6.3). This addresses the concern I raised in the first round about distinguishing teleological song form from non-teleological loop structures.
4. Clarity:
Somewhat clear
Comments/Explanation:
The revision is better structured than the original. The Discussion is now organised around three explicit research questions (RQ1, RQ2, RQ3), which gives the argument a clearer spine. The distinction between what chord types appear and how they are sequenced (Section 6.2) is a useful framing.Two issues remain.First, the paper now distinguishes between chord vocabulary and sequential practice (Section 6.2), which is welcome. A brief orienting paragraph would sharpen this further by separating what each metric captures: chord-quality distributions describe harmonic palette; trigram frequencies and correlations describe local sequential behaviour; Axis rates describe membership in a canonical four-chord family; and BPE merge histories describe recurring longer patterns. The high correlation between Udio and Last.fm trigram frequencies (r = 0.952), for example, tells us about distributional similarity but says nothing about whether the progressions occupy the same formal positions or occur at comparable rates of harmonic change.Second, the hedging around 'algorithmic monoculture' in Section 6.4 is improved, but the prose remains evaluatively loaded. Although the response letter presents the framing as descriptive, the manuscript still says that Suno 'exemplifies this pattern' and suggests that such platforms prioritise 'the production of digital files, as a gamification process, over the exploration of musical knowledge'. That language is stronger than the evidence requires. Many highly valued musical traditions operate within restricted harmonic vocabularies; twelve-bar blues, Ramones-era punk, minimal techno, and indeed much of the 'chill-out' music that Suno's 'Jazz' tag seems to describe. A brief acknowledgement that harmonic restriction is not inherently a compositional deficiency would bring the discussion into better balance.
5. Reproducibility:
Reproducibility remains a clear strength. The release of beat-level chord annotations, structural boundaries, metadata, and code under an MIT licence aligns with best practices. The adoption of publicly available, unmodified pretrained models (Poltronieri et al. 2025; Kim and Nam 2023) enhances reproducibility compared to the original submission's custom pipeline.The reliance on proprietary AI platforms continues to introduce reproducibility challenges, as Suno and Udio update their models frequently. The results reported here apply to Suno versions 3.5 and below. This is appropriately acknowledged, and the authors' suggestion that the analysis be repeated as models are updated (Section 7) is well taken.
6. Overall value:
Medium high
Comments/Explanation:
The revision represents a substantial and conscientious reworking of the manuscript. The principal methodological concerns from the first round have been addressed, and the restructured Discussion, organised around three explicit research questions, is more coherent than the original.From a musicological perspective, the paper now engages more seriously with the analytical literature. The addition of Richards (2017) and Biamonte (2010) provides appropriate theoretical grounding, and the explicit acknowledgement of voice leading, harmonic rhythm, and chord-voicing detail as scope limitations (Section 6.1) is honest and welcome. The teleological/non-teleological form distinction raised in Section 6.3 addresses a question that is central to understanding what AI-generated formal structure actually represents.A question worth raising for future work is what the statistical patterns documented here mean for our understanding of harmonic practice more broadly. The paper establishes that Suno converges on canonical pop progressions at 2.5 to 4.6 times the rate found in human-composed music; what this convergence tells us about the relationship between harmonic convention, compositional agency, and 'diversity' in popular music remains an open and productive question.The remaining revisions I am suggesting are focused and should not require fundamental reconceptualisation. (1) A sentence on the all-orderings assumption in the Axis analysis. (2) A brief acknowledgement that harmonic restriction is not inherently a deficiency. (3) A brief orienting paragraph distinguishing what the various harmonic metrics capture.
7. Ethical approval:
No human subjects were used. The authors address data acquisition ethics in Section 9, invoking the EU Copyright Directive's text-and-data-mining exception for their web scraping of Suno and Udio content.
8. Other Comments:
The observation that Suno/Udio 'Jazz' lacks standard jazz harmonic patterns deserves more analytical attention than it currently receives. If Suno's 'Jazz' output is harmonically simple, this may reflect the semantic content of user prompts ('chill', 'relaxing', 'background') rather than any limitation of the model's harmonic capacity. The paper's framing treats the model's output as evidence of what it can produce, but user prompts are a powerful confound. Without access to prompt data (which the authors do not have), it is impossible to disentangle model capacity from user intent. A sentence noting this ambiguity would be valuable.One further point. The paper reports that Suno's harmonic vocabulary comprises 6,770 unique trigram types compared to 8,056 in Udio and 9,838 in Last.fm (Section 6.2). These numbers are informative, but they should be interpreted cautiously. The Last.fm collection draws from a human catalogue spanning decades, genres, and cultural contexts; Suno's collection is the output of a single model over a limited period. Comparing vocabulary sizes across such different generative contexts is meaningful, but the asymmetry should be explicitly flagged.I commend the authors for the scope and thoroughness of their revision. The paper is significantly stronger than the original submission.
