\begin{abstract}
We present a large-scale comparison of the formal and harmonic structures of music generated by users of two AI music generation platforms, Suno and Udio, against human-composed popular music. We address three questions: 1) How does AI-generated music's harmony relate to human popular music? 2) What structural similarities or differences exist? 3) What considerations arise when applying music information retrieval (MIR) tools to AI-generated music?
We analyze 20,002 Suno tracks, 20,002 Udio tracks, and 20,010 human-composed recordings. Harmonic analysis uses a state-of-the-art conformer-based chord estimation model; structural analysis uses a joint beat-tracking and semantic section labeling model (All-In-One). We model chord progressions as trigrams and apply byte-pair encoding to section-label sequences to quantify formal diversity across the three collections.
Analysis of over 2.52 million chord trigrams reveals that Suno concentrates its harmony on Western pop patterns such as \texttt{I--V--vi--IV}, while Udio exhibits more balanced chord distributions resembling human-composed music. Byte-pair encoding analysis shows Suno tends toward simple verse-chorus repetitions, whereas Udio exhibits the widest structural variety. These findings suggest that Suno (versions 3.5 and below) converges on a narrow harmonic and formal space, whereas Udio exhibits tendencies closer to human-composed music. We release beat-level chord annotations, metadata, section boundaries, self-similarity matrices, audio links, and code under an MIT license.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Commercial AI music generation platforms are becoming popular, and the resulting music is beginning to flood streaming services \citep{Sturm2024a}. Suno,\endnote{\url{https://suno.com}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.} launched online in late 2023, claimed in November 2024 to have a user base of 25 million people.\endnote{\url{http://bit.ly/4nnZA55}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.} Udio,\endnote{\url{https://www.udio.com}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.}
launched in the Spring of 2024, reported soon after that its users were generating ten songs a second.\endnote{\url{https://bit.ly/46QOGyy}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.}
The summer of 2025 witnessed the appearance of an AI-generated music album by the fake ``indie rock band'' {\em The Velvet Sundown}, which has received millions of streams on Spotify.\endnote{\url{https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/jul/14/an-ai-generated-band-got-1m-plays-on-spotify-now-music-insiders-say-listeners-should-be-warned}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.}
The French music streaming service Deezer announced in September 2025 that they are receiving $30,000$ ``fully AI-generated tracks daily''.\endnote{\url{https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/nearly-a-third-of-all-tracks-uploaded-to-deezer-are-now-fully-ai-generated-says-platform/}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.}

This growing catalog of AI-generated music raises several interesting questions for musicology and presents challenges for music information retrieval (MIR). How does the harmonic content of AI-generated music relate to the harmonic practices found in human-composed music? What structural similarities and differences are there between AI-generated and human-composed music? Given the rate at which this catalog is growing, how reliable and scalable are the computational methods of MIR for addressing these questions?

In this article, we address these questions specifically in the context of AI-generated music, drawing on examples from Suno and Udio. We describe the creation of a large corpus of $60,014$ music recordings, balanced across three sources: $20,002$ songs from Suno users, $20,002$ songs from Udio users, and $20,010$ human-composed Western popular songs.

Recent advances in MIR have made corpus-scale harmonic and structural analysis of popular music increasingly viable. Even though the challenges of resolving chord and structural information across diverse musical types remain an open problem, we employ consonance-ACE \citep{poltronieri2025discord} for harmonic analysis and All-In-One \citep{taejun2023allinone} for structural analysis, two recent implementations that bring the analysis significantly closer to musicological utility. The results of our harmonic analyses reveal that music from Suno exhibits strong stylistic characteristics of Western pop chord progressions, whereas music from Udio exhibits more balanced chord-quality distributions, resembling those found in human-composed music. The results of our structural analyses show that music from Suno tends toward simple AB (verse-chorus) repetition, whereas music from Udio exhibits a wider range of structural variations.

In summary, our contributions are:
(i) a large comparative analysis of harmonic and formal properties in human-made and AI-generated music using a consistent pipeline,
(ii) publicly released annotations for $60,014$ tracks, including segmentations, beat-level chord estimates, style tags \citep{oramas2018multimodal}, and audio metadata, and (iii) open-sourced code to reproduce the experiments.\endnote{\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/Aimir_Structure_Analysis}}
The remainder is organized as follows. The next section reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:corpora} describes the corpora used in the study. Section~\ref{sec:method} motivates and describes our pipeline for harmonic and formal analysis. Section~\ref{sec:trigram_comparisons} presents our results. Finally, Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications of our work, its limitations, and future directions.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

The computational analysis of musical structure and harmony is a focus of MIR \citep{Muller2015a}.

\subsection{AI Music Generation}
Recent open-source work on text-conditioned music and audio synthesis includes models such as MusicGen \citep{copet2023simple}, MusicLM \citep{agostinelli2023musiclm}, and Stable Audio Open \citep{evans2025stable}, representing the state of the art in publicly available generative audio research. However, the focus of this paper is not on the generative models themselves, but on the musical properties of their output, analyzed at the corpus scale in comparison with human-produced music.

\subsubsection{Evaluation of AI-Generated Music}
\citet{Sturm2016c} evaluates \textit{folk-rnn}, a generative system trained on a large corpus of folk tunes in ABC notation, examining outputs in relation to musical practice rather than only automatic metrics, with a concrete evaluation pipeline, corpus statistics analysis, and focused musical inspection to locate where model outputs resemble and where they diverge from human repertoire. \citet{Ariza2009a} shows that interrogator-style authorship discrimination tests only establish that a digital music artifact sounds plausible to listeners, which is insufficient to characterize the musical quality produced by an AI system.

\citet{dervakos2021heuristics} propose a framework for evaluating symbolic AI-generated music using tools from music theory, defining heuristic metrics grounded in tonic coordinate systems and tone networks, such as the circle of fifths, to quantify musicality without relying on user surveys or real data.

\citet{wu2020jazz} analyzes the outputs of the Jazz Transformer using objective metrics, including pitch-class histogram entropy, chord-progression irregularity, groove-pattern similarity, and structureness indicators, demonstrating that systematic shortcomings in AI-composed music are not fully captured by listening tests alone. Notably, their definition of chord progression irregularity as the proportion of unique chord trigrams in a piece establishes a precedent for trigram-based harmonic analysis of AI-generated music.

Some studies find biased perception of music when works are labeled as AI \citep{Ragot2020t,Shank2022q,Deguernel2023a}, others report null effects \citep{Pasquier2016a,Zlatkov2023a}. Together, these motivate a careful, data-driven comparison between music made with current AI platforms and music created by humans.

\subsection{Music Structure Analysis}
\label{sec:msa}
Music Structure Analysis (MSA) is the task of segmenting an audio recording into contiguous, non-overlapping sections and assigning labels that reflect musical function or similarity \citep{nieto2020audio}. \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{Computational approaches exploit four main principles: homogeneity (sections internally consistent with respect to some musical attribute), repetition (detecting recurrent sequences), novelty (identifying boundary points where musical content changes abruptly), and regularity (statistical properties of segment durations). Classical methods relied on self-similarity matrices and novelty curves derived from hand-crafted features such as chromagrams and MFCCs \citep{foote2000automatic}, repetition and recurrence-based representations \citep{levy2008structural, serra2012unsupervised}, Laplacian spectral decomposition \citep{mcfee2014learning}, and barwise self-similarity with dynamic programming \citep{Marmoret_2023}.} Despite these advances, MSA remains an open challenge due to the subjectivity of structural annotations: the best reported structure labeling scores reach approximately 74\% on the Harmonix Set \citep{taejun2023allinone}, well below the estimated human inter-rater agreement of approximately 90\% \citep{nieto2020audio}.

More recent approaches have improved structural analysis through deep learning, including few-shot embeddings for Laplacian decomposition \citep{salamon2021deep}, unsupervised contrastive multi-level representations \citep{buisson2022learning}, \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{self-supervised novelty detection \citep{peeters2023self}, hierarchical spectral-temporal Transformers for direct semantic labeling \citep{wang2022catch}, and graph attention networks for pairwise structure prediction \citep{buisson2024using}.}

For functional structure extraction, we employ the All-In-One model \citep{taejun2023allinone}, which jointly performs beat tracking, downbeat detection, segment boundary detection, and semantic section labeling (verse, chorus, bridge, etc.) in a single architecture. We selected it because it directly outputs the section labels our analysis requires and achieves state-of-the-art performance on the Harmonix Set across all four tasks. The model is described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:form_pipeline}.

\subsection{Automatic Chord Estimation}
\label{sec:ace}
Automatic Chord Estimation (ACE) is a central task in MIR with applications in music performance, transcription, and formal analysis research. ACE has evolved from early template and chroma-based systems with probabilistic sequence models, such as Hidden Markov Models, Conditional Random Fields, or Deep Belief Networks \citep{mauch2009simultaneous, ni2012end, humphrey2015four}, to more recent deep learning approaches that learn end-to-end representations from spectral features, increasingly targeting large chord vocabularies that include sevenths, extensions, and inversions.

\highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{Large-vocabulary ACE has advanced through several complementary strategies.} \citet{mcfee2017structured} decouple pitch-class detection from label assignment in a 170-class framework; \citet{jiang2019large} decompose chord labels into independent musical components (root, quality, bass, extensions), generalizing to 301+ classes; and \citet{park2019bi} propose BTC, a bi-directional Transformer achieving competitive large-vocabulary performance with publicly available code. Most recently, \citet{poltronieri2025discord} propose \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{consonance-ACE}, a Conformer-based model whose decomposed decoder reconstructs chord labels without a fixed vocabulary, paired with consonance-based label smoothing, outperforming BTC on both standard and non-binary evaluation metrics on RWC Pop and USPop. \citet{waseem2025chordformer} further extends this line with ChordFormer for large-vocabulary recognition.

\highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{Despite these advances, accessible implementations remain limited in coverage.} Open-source libraries such as Madmom \citep{bock2016madmom} and librosa \citep{mcfee2025librosa} provide accessible implementations of baseline chord estimation algorithms, most of which remain fundamentally limited to detecting major and minor triads, struggling with tetrads, extensions, suspensions, and inversions. Earlier efforts to expand chord vocabularies, such as \textit{Chordino} \citep{mauch2010approximate}, while still functional as a Vamp plugin, suffer from platform- and dependency-compatibility issues; \highLight[r2!20!white]{the library is no longer maintained and cannot be integrated into modern Python pipelines.} \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{Furthermore, analyses relying on chord-class aggregation can misrepresent stylistic progressions: \citet{collins2025recording} demonstrate that \textit{Chordino}-based histograms failed to capture modal-interchange progressions while detecting only broad palette differences. Multi-source approaches such as DECIBEL \citep{odekerken2020decibel}, which fuse audio features with crowd-sourced MIDI annotations and guitar tablature, partially address vocabulary restrictions, yielding improvements of 0.5--13.6 percentage points over audio-only baselines on $180$ Beatles songs and $20$ Queen songs from the Isophonics chord-annotation corpus\endnote{\url{http://isophonics.net/content/reference-annotations}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.}, though major and minor accuracy typically remains between 75\% and 87\%, a ceiling attributed to annotation subjectivity.}

Roman Numeral Analysis (RNA) encodes chords relative to a tonal center, but benchmarks on the PARC dataset \citep{poppe2025polyphonic} confirm that even state-of-the-art models achieve modest accuracy on real polyphonic audio, particularly for complex progressions.

Given these considerations, this study adopts \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{consonance-ACE} \citep{poltronieri2025discord} for three reasons: (1) its decomposed decoder is not constrained to a fixed vocabulary, accommodating harmonic diversity beyond major/minor triads; (2) consonance-based label smoothing produces errors that fall between harmonically related chords rather than random ones; and (3) its demonstrated superiority over BTC on standard benchmarks, combined with publicly available code, provides a reproducible foundation for large-scale analysis. The model is described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:ace_pipeline}.

\highLight[r2!20!white]{\subsection{Harmony Analysis of Corpora}
Several annotated corpora and computational analyses have contributed to the study of harmony and musical structure in popular music. A hand-annotated corpus of rock songs provides detailed harmonic and melodic analysis, enabling statistical investigation of chords, scale degrees, and key-finding models~\citep{temperley2013statistical}. Expert ground-truth chord annotations for charting songs support research in automatic chord recognition and music analysis~\citep{burgoyne2011expert}. Computational analysis of Billboard chart data has been used to study the evolution of harmony, timbre, and style in popular music from 1960 to 2010~\citep{mauch2015evolution}. Long-term trends in harmonic and timbral features of contemporary Western popular music have also been examined to document changes in musical diversity~\citep{serra2012measuring}. Automated approaches to chord and key estimation provide frameworks for the simultaneous identification of chords and musical context from audio data~\citep{mauch2009simultaneous}. However, there is no comprehensive corpus-level comparison of harmonic and structural properties between current AI platforms and human music.}

\section{Corpora}
\label{sec:corpora}

The corpora are formed as a collection of three audio datasets (summarised in Table \ref{tab:datasets}) containing audio files from \textit{Suno} and \textit{Udio}, from the AI-generated side, and human-generated music found on Lastfm catalog. The audio tracks originate from \citep{cros2025ai}, where web scraping was performed for the \textit{Suno} and \textit{Udio} datasets between May and October 2024. \textit{Suno} data was collected from its frequently updated "New Songs" playlist \endnote{\url{https://suno.com/playlist/cc14084a-2622-4c4b-8258-1f6b4b4f54b3}, last accessed Sep. 30, 2025.}, queried every two hours to capture its daily influx of $50$ new tracks. \highLight[r2!20!white]{\textit{Udio} data was collected from the platform's publicly accessible front page.} Both datasets include metadata and audio files. The audio file folder contained more than $200,000$ files, from which we randomly selected approximately $20,000$ audio files from each dataset for analysis. The corpora uses the Million Song Dataset (MSD) \citep{Bertin-Mahieux2011}\endnote{\url{https://github.com/renesemela/lastfm-dataset-2020/raw/master/datasets/lastfm_dataset_2020/lastfm_dataset_2020.db}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.} to confirm the audio files are human-created music, leveraging Lastfm's associated YouTube URLs to download around $200,000$ unique tracks, where we randomly chose $20,010$.\endnote{We used \texttt{yt-dlp} \url{https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp}, last accessed Sep. 30 2025.} We thus refer to this collection as ``\textit{Lastfm}''.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
This section describes the pipeline used to extract harmonic and structural information from the $60,014$ audio recordings in our corpus, and the derived representations for comparative analysis.

\subsection{Automatic Chord Estimation}
\label{sec:ace_pipeline}
Chord estimation is performed using \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{consonance-ACE} \citep{poltronieri2025discord}, which independently predicts root, bass, and pitch-class activations and reconstructs chord labels symbolically through a decomposed decoder. The model operates on CQT spectrograms computed over 20-second chunks and outputs frame-level chord predictions with no constraint to a fixed vocabulary. We use the publicly available pretrained model without modification. Chord segments shorter than 0.5 seconds are merged into adjacent segments, and consecutive frames with identical chord labels are collapsed into continuous intervals.

The output of the chord estimator consists of absolute chord labels (e.g., \texttt{D:min7}, \texttt{G:7}, \texttt{C:maj7}) aligned with a time onset-offset columns as shown in Table \ref{tab:ace_output}. To enable key-relative analysis, we convert these to functional harmony representations (e.g., \texttt{ii}, \texttt{V}, \texttt{I}). For each track, we first estimate the global key by computing the pitch-class distribution from the chord-root sequence and correlating it with the major- and minor-key profiles \citep{krumhansl1982tracing}. Each absolute chord label is then mapped to its corresponding Roman numeral degree relative to the estimated key using the \texttt{music21} library \citep{cuthbert2010music21}. Consecutive identical Roman numerals are deduplicated before downstream n-gram analysis. \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{The resulting chord intervals are subsequently aligned to beat positions estimated by the All-In-One model (Section~\ref{sec:form_pipeline}), yielding the beat-level chord annotations included in the released dataset.}

\subsection{Musical Form Estimation}
\label{sec:form_pipeline}
Musical structure is extracted using the All-In-One model \citep{taejun2023allinone}, which jointly performs four tasks in a single architecture: beat tracking (locating regular pulse positions), downbeat detection (identifying the first beat of each bar), segment boundary detection (finding time points where sections change), and functional structure labelling (assigning semantic labels such as verse, chorus, bridge, intro, outro, or solo to each segment).

The model first separates the input audio into four stems (drums, bass, vocals, other) using Hybrid Transformer Demucs \citep{rouard2023hybrid} and processes the resulting spectrograms through a stack of transformer modules combining dilated and neighbourhood attention. Four output heads produce frame-level predictions for each task. The model achieves state-of-the-art performance on the Harmonix Set (F1 = 0.958 for beats, F1 = 0.915 for downbeats, HR.5F = 0.660 for segmentation, PWF = 0.738 for structure labelling).

We apply the publicly available pretrained model without modification. For each track, the model outputs a sequence of labelled sections with their onset and offset times, stored as \texttt{\_musical\_form.lab} files. Consecutive identical section labels are subsequently merged before downstream analysis.

% Additionally, self-similarity matrix (SSM) representations and Laplacian spectral decompositions were computed for each track following \citet{mcfee2014learning}. These provide complementary unsupervised structural descriptions (segment boundaries and cluster labels) without semantic interpretation. Full details are provided in the supplementary material, and the SSM-derived annotations are included in the released dataset.

\subsection{Trigram Dataset Generation}
\label{sec:trigram_generation}
To systematically analyse harmonic tendencies across collections, we constructed a \textit{trigram dataset}---a corpus of all consecutive three-chord sequences---extracted from each collection's data.
For example, the progression $Dm7$, $Ab7$, $Gm7$, $C\#ø7$, \ldots\ yields $[Dm7, Ab7, Gm7]$, $[Ab7, Gm7, C\#ø7]$, and so on.
The process is as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \highLight[r2!20!white]{\textbf{Normalization:} Absolute chord labels are first converted to Roman numerals and consecutive identical Roman numerals are deduplicated, as described in Section~\ref{sec:ace_pipeline}. The example above uses absolute chord labels for illustrative clarity; all trigrams used in analysis are extracted from the deduplicated Roman numeral sequences. Sequences containing ``no-chord'' labels were not considered for trigrams.}

    \item \textbf{Trigram Generation:}
    For each song, we extracted overlapping chord trigrams from the \highLight[r2!20!white]{Roman numeral sequence} using a sliding window approach:
    \[
        (C_{i},\ C_{i+1},\ C_{i+2}), \quad i = 1, \ldots, N-2
    \]
    where $N$ is the number of chord events in the song \highLight[r2!20!white]{after deduplication}, and $C_i$ denotes the $i^{th}$ \highLight[r2!20!white]{Roman numeral}. All trigrams were retained, including repetitions and sequences spanning sections or boundaries, unless a song or section was shorter than three chords.
    
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Byte-Pair Encoding of Harmonic and Formal Sequences}
\label{sec:bpe}

To compare structural and harmonic diversity across collections, we apply Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) \citep{sennrich2016neural} to two types of sequences. BPE is a subword tokenization algorithm that iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent symbol pairs in a corpus, building a vocabulary of recurring patterns from the bottom up.

For \textbf{musical form}, \highLight[r2!20!white]{each track's section sequence is first converted into overlapping trigrams of consecutive section labels (e.g.\ \texttt{verse·chorus·verse}), and BPE is applied using each trigram as the basic unit.} \highLight[r2!20!white]{BPE is run for up to 80 merge operations per collection, which is sufficient to exhaust all meaningful merges given the small section-label vocabulary.}

For \textbf{harmonic sequences}, we first extract chord trigrams from the Roman numeral sequences produced by the ACE pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:ace_pipeline}). A trigram is a window of three consecutive, mutually distinct Roman numeral symbols. Each track's sequence is represented as a series of trigrams, and BPE is applied at the trigram level: each token fed to the algorithm is itself a trigram, and merge operations identify the most frequent pairs of adjacent trigrams. \highLight[r2!20!white]{BPE is run for 150 merge operations per collection, an empirical cap chosen to capture the most frequent recurring patterns without over-merging sparse ones.} The resulting merge history, restricted to base-to-base merges (i.e., pairs in which both tokens are single trigrams), reveals the most common harmonic progressions of \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{four} chords and their cross-collection frequency distributions.

\subsection{Axis of Awesome Chord Progression Analysis}
\label{sec:axis}

We measure how often songs in each collection contain chord progressions matching the Axis of Awesome \citep{richards2017tonal}, using the Roman numeral sequences produced by the ACE pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:ace_pipeline}). Four-chord sequences are formed as \textbf{p-trigrams}, pairs of adjacent chord trigrams, consistent with the trigram vocabulary used throughout this paper (Section~\ref{sec:bpe}).

The Axis of Awesome is associated with three four-chord sets: the major form \{I, V, vi, IV\}, the minor form \{i, $\flat$VI, III, $\flat$VII\}, and a minor variant \{i, $\flat$VI, $\flat$III, $\flat$VII\}. We treat all 24 orderings of each set as valid match targets, giving 72 distinct four-chord patterns in total. \highLight[r3!20!white]{This decision is deliberate: the analysis targets harmonic vocabulary membership, whether a track draws on axis-family pitch material, rather than the syntactic function of any specific ordering. As \citet{richards2017tonal} discusses, rotations of the same four-chord set can carry different tonal implications; the reported axis rates should therefore be read as a measure of axis-family prevalence, not as evidence of a single recurring functional progression.} From here, we will refer to this pattern of chord progressions as Axis.

All Roman numeral labels are first normalized enharmonically by mapping each label to its semitone position and back (e.g., \texttt{\#V}~$\rightarrow$~$\flat$\texttt{VI}), and consecutive identical labels are removed. Trigrams are then extracted from each collapsed sequence using a sliding window of three chords; only trigrams in which all three chords are distinct are retained, consistent with the BPE trigram definition. Adjacent trigram pairs are formed from the retained list: a pair $(\langle a,b,c \rangle,\, \langle b,c,d \rangle)$ is valid only if the second trigram starts exactly one position after the first in the original sequence (i.e., they share a two-chord overlap). Each valid pair reconstructs a four-chord window $(a, b, c, d)$, which is tested for exact membership in the set of 72 axis patterns.

The \textbf{p-trigram axis rate} for a song is the proportion of valid p-trigram pairs whose reconstructed window matches any axis pattern, expressed as a percentage.

\subsection{Harmonic Tendencies}
\label{sec:suno_tendencies}
\highLight[r2!20!white]{Figure~\ref{fig:pop_prevalence} shows the relative frequency of canonical pop/rock chord trigrams --- defined as permutations of \{I/i, IV/iv, V/v, vi/VI/$\flat$VI\} --- in each collection, computed as risk ratios against Lastfm and Udio using the method described in Section~\ref{sec:trigram_generation}.} The figure demonstrates that Suno tends to favor chord progressions strongly associated with the Western pop idiom, particularly those prevalent since the early 2000s. For every progression tested, Suno's relative frequency is significantly greater than that observed in the other collections ($p < 0.001$ for all comparisons). As shown in ~\citep{temperley2013statistical}, the centrality of progressions such as \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{I--V--vi--IV}}, \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{IV--I--V}}, and related idioms is a defining feature of Western pop/rock music; they report that the most frequent non-tonic chord roots are \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{IV}}, \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{V}}, \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{bVII}}, and \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{vi}}, with progressions among these chords forming the core stylistic harmonic vocabulary. Our results both replicate and extend these findings, revealing the extent to which such progressions are even more prevalent in AI-generated music from Suno.

To quantify the tendencies shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:trigrams_per_style} for each canonical progression in Suno relative to the reference collections (Lastfm and Udio), we calculated a \emph{risk ratio (RR)} for every trigram.  Raw counts are misleading because Suno contains far more trigrams than the reference corpus, so the RR normalizes by each collection's total:

\[
\operatorname{RR}(t) \;=\;
\frac{N_{\mathrm{Suno},t}/N_{\mathrm{Suno}}}
     {N_{\mathrm{Ref},t}/N_{\mathrm{Ref}}}
\]

\noindent
where $N_{\mathrm{Suno},t}$ and $N_{\mathrm{Ref},t}$ are the counts of trigram~$t$ in \textit{Suno} and in the reference corpus, respectively, while $N_{\mathrm{Suno}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{Ref}}$ denote the total numbers of trigrams in those two corpora.

\smallskip
To test whether the difference is greater than expected by chance, we build a $2\times2$ contingency table (\emph{Suno vs.\ Reference}\,$\times$\,$t$ vs.\ ``all other trigrams'') and apply Fisher's exact test. All raw $p$-values are corrected for multiple comparisons with the Benjamini--Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure \citep{benjamini1995controlling}; the resulting $p$-value is used to filter and choose the trigrams that are shown in the Table \ref{tab:suno_enrichment}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item at least $30$ occurrences in Suno and $10$ in the reference,
  \item risk ratio $RR\ge2$,
  \item adjusted $p-$value $ < 0.01$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
                 & Target trigram & Other trigrams \\\hline
\textbf{Suno}    & $a$            & $b$ \\
\textbf{Reference} & $c$            & $d$
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent where \(a=N_{\text{Suno},t}\), \(b=N_{\text{Suno}}-a\), \(c=N_{\text{Ref},t}\), and \(d=N_{\text{Ref}}-c\).
A risk ratio $RR_t > 1$ together with $p< 0.01$ indicates that trigram $t$ is significantly more common in Suno than in the reference corpus, as is the case for all the trigrams shown.

\subsubsection{Summary}

The results from our BPE analysis suggest several insights relevant to broader questions about the formal structures and creative capacities of AI-generated music.

First, we observe that Suno exhibits a strong preference for simple, repetitive structures, particularly repeated verse-chorus cycles. This convergence toward a reduced formal vocabulary likely reflects biases in generation objectives optimised for familiarity and accessibility, as well as the short song snippets to be extended later via the web interface. Udio shows similar structural tendencies but with merge frequencies closer to Lastfm. A key confound for both platforms is song duration: Suno tracks average 175 seconds (approximately 2.9 minutes), Udio tracks average 143 seconds (approximately 2.4 minutes), and Lastfm tracks average 261 seconds (approximately 4.4 minutes), making AI-generated tracks roughly half to two-thirds the length of their human-composed counterparts. The reduced structural diversity observed in Suno and Udio may therefore reflect not only a bias toward formulaic composition but also a constraint imposed by shorter generation lengths, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:durations}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% Explain this is an attempt at analysing the musical aspects and musical form of AI vs Humans' music by implementing a state-of-the-art MIR tool. 
% Discuss the question: 

This study addresses three research questions about AI-generated music at corpus scale:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1] How reliably can current MIR tools analyze harmonic structure, vocabulary, and musical form in AI-generated music?
  \item[RQ2] To what extent does the harmony of AI-generated music relate to human-composed music?
  \item[RQ3] What structural similarities or differences emerge when comparing AI-generated and human-composed music?
\end{description}

\noindent We discuss each in turn, then draw out two cross-cutting implications: outcome homogenization and creative agency (Section~\ref{sec:homogenization}).

\subsection{RQ1: Applying MIR Tools to AI-Generated Music}
\label{sec:disc_mir}

The chord estimator and structural model adopted in this study (Sections~\ref{sec:ace_pipeline} and~\ref{sec:form_pipeline}) address the principal methodological concerns for corpus-scale analysis, but challenges remain that should be acknowledged. For chord estimation, even state-of-the-art models exhibit lower accuracy on complex chord types (sevenths, extensions, suspensions, inversions) than on triads~\citep{pauwels2013evaluating}, and reported performance is sensitive to evaluation vocabulary and chord mapping strategies. Annotations from the Polyphonic Audio to Roman Corpus confirm that current RNA models achieve modest accuracy on real polyphonic audio, especially for harmonically complex progressions~\citep{poppe2025polyphonic}. In our pipeline, the conversion from absolute chord labels to Roman numerals depends on reliable global key estimation via pitch-class correlation against Krumhansl-Kessler profiles. While this method is standard, key estimation errors propagate to every downstream Roman numeral label in a given track. For tracks with key ambiguity, modulation, or modal mixture, the functional analysis may misrepresent the intended harmonic relationships. \textit{Music21} library also required normalization of chord names to form the functional related chord. \highLight[r2!20!white]{Furthermore, consonance-ACE assumes enharmonic equivalence: sharp ninths, sharp elevenths, and flat thirteenths are encoded as their enharmonic equivalents (flat third, flat fifth, and flat sixth respectively), and therefore do not appear as distinct labels in the chord vocabulary.}

This study operates at the level of chord-label distributions and n-gram sequential patterns, which is the appropriate granularity for corpus-scale comparison across 60,014 tracks. Voice leading, harmonic rhythm, and chord-voicing detail are not captured in any chord-symbol-based corpus study and fall outside the present scope.

For structural analysis, the All-In-One model provides semantic section labels (verse, chorus, bridge, intro, outro) unavailable from unsupervised methods. Its reported scores on the Harmonix Set (HR.5F = 0.660 for segmentation, PWF = 0.738 for structure labeling) remain below estimated human inter-rater agreement~\citep{nieto2020audio}, so a degree of error is present in the annotations. However, since the same model is applied uniformly across all three collections, these errors affect each corpus equally. Our analysis targets distributional tendencies across 60,014 tracks; systematic differences between collections therefore reflect genuine structural tendencies rather than tool-specific artifacts.

Despite these caveats, the combination of a chord estimator with a semantically aware structure analyzer represents a meaningful advance over the triad-level, label-free pipelines that were the practical default for large-scale audio analysis until recently. The rapid development of both ACE~\citep{poltronieri2025discord, park2019bi, mcfee2017structured, jiang2019large, waseem2025chordformer} and MSA~\citep{taejun2023allinone, wang2022catch, buisson2024using, peeters2023self} tools, with publicly released code and pretrained models, makes corpus-scale harmonic and formal analysis of this kind increasingly feasible.

\subsection{RQ2: Harmonic Relationship Between AI-Generated and Human-Composed Music}
\label{sec:disc_harmony}

\highLight[r3!20!white]{The four metrics used in this section capture different dimensions of harmonic practice and should be read accordingly. Chord-quality distributions (Figure~\ref{fig:vocabulary_distribution}) describe the harmonic palette, which chord types appear and how often. Trigram frequencies and cross-collection correlations describe local sequential behavior, in which three-chord successions recur and at what relative rates. Axis rates measure membership in a canonical four-chord family, not the prevalence of any single ordering. BPE merge histories describe recurring, longer patterns, across which tetragrams and extended sequences are consolidated within a collection. These dimensions are complementary but independent: a high trigram-frequency correlation between two collections, such as the $r = 0.952$ shared by Udio and Lastfm, indicates distributional similarity in local harmonic motion but says nothing about whether those progressions occupy the same formal positions within a song or occur at comparable rates of harmonic change.}

Three strands of evidence converge on a consistent picture of how AI-generated harmony relates to human-composed music.

At the level of chord-quality vocabulary, the three collections are broadly similar: major triads dominate, followed by \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{minor triads,} power chords, seventh chords, dominant sevenths, and suspended chords (Figure~\ref{fig:vocabulary_distribution}). This indicates that current generative platforms reproduce the surface-level harmonic palette of popular music with fidelity. The differences emerge not in what chord types appear, but in how they are sequenced.

Trigram frequency analysis reveals that Suno concentrates its harmonic motion on a narrow subset of Western pop progressions. Across all 53~canonical progressions defined as permutations of \{I/i, IV/iv, V/v, vi/VI/$\flat$VI\} that pass the enrichment filters (FDR-adjusted $p \ll 0.001$, RR~$\geq 1.3$), Suno is overrepresented at frequency ratios ranging from $1.3\times$ to $3.3\times$ relative to Lastfm. The highest-frequency 15 of these 53~progressions, listed in Panel~A of Table~\ref{tab:suno_enrichment}, concentrate the over-representation at $2.1\times$--$3.3\times$, while the remaining lower-frequency canonical progressions contribute ratios in the $1.3\times$--$2.1\times$ range. The cumulative effect across all 53 enriched progressions is substantial: Suno's harmonic vocabulary is contracted to 6,770 unique trigram types, compared to 8,056 in Udio and 9,838 in Lastfm.

The Axis of Awesome analysis extends this picture to the four-chord level. At the $\geq$10\% axis p-trigram threshold, 32.0\% of Suno songs qualify as axis-based, versus 12.6\% in Lastfm and 14.7\% in Udio (Table~\ref{tab:axis_classification}). At the strictest level ($\geq$50\% of harmonic windows matching the Axis loop), Suno reaches 10.5\% of songs, approximately $4.6\times$ the rate observed in Lastfm (2.3\%) and $3.5\times$ that of Udio (3.0\%). Songs built on canonical axis-family patterns are thus present at roughly 2.5 to 4.6 times the rate found in the human reference corpus, depending on the strictness of the threshold applied.

Udio's harmonic distributions, by contrast, are statistically closer to those of Lastfm across all three measures. Cross-collection trigram frequency correlations confirm this: Udio and Lastfm share $r = 0.952$, while Suno correlates with Lastfm at only $r = 0.441$. The BPE merge histories of harmonic sequences (Figures~\ref{fig:bpe_trigrams_suno}) reinforce this at the tetragram level, with Udio and Lastfm sharing similar merge-pair frequency profiles while Suno diverges toward a smaller set of high-frequency patterns. Udio features minor-key functional progressions (e.g., \texttt{bVI-bVII-i}, \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{i-bVI-III}}, \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{i-bVII-bVI}}) among its most frequent trigrams (see Figure~\ref{fig:top_30_trigrams}). Progressions of this type, particularly the \highLight[r2!20!white]{\texttt{bVI--bVII--i}} pattern, have been identified as characteristic harmonic idioms of rock music, functioning as Aeolian cadences with subdominant--dominant--tonic implications distinct from conventional major-minor tonality~\citep{biamonte2010triadic}. Lastfm's top trigrams are dominated by functional major progressions (\texttt{IV-I-V}, \texttt{IV-V-I}, \texttt{V-IV-I}), with minor-mode patterns present but appearing further down the frequency ranking (see Figure~\ref{fig:top_30_trigrams}).

\subsection{RQ3: Structural Similarities and Differences}
\label{sec:disc_structure}

The BPE analysis of section-label sequences and the Unique Tokens Ratio provide complementary views of formal diversity across the three collections.

Suno exhibits the strongest convergence toward simple, repetitive structures. Verse-chorus cycles (ABAB patterns) dominate its merge history, and its Unique Tokens Ratio is the lowest of the three collections ($0.44 \pm 0.18$), indicating that the average Suno track draws from fewer distinct section types than either human-composed or Udio-generated music. Udio, by contrast, shows the highest formal diversity ($0.58 \pm 0.20$), with Lastfm occupying an intermediate position ($0.51 \pm 0.19$). The BPE merge-pair heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:BPE_musical_form}) confirms that Udio's merge frequencies are closer to Lastfm than to Suno. \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{Of particular note, the second most frequent pattern in both AI collections --- \texttt{verse·chorus·inst~$\rightarrow$~verse} (Suno: 2,103; Udio: 1,599) --- is entirely absent from Lastfm. Both systems frequently produce instrumental sections as structural connectors within verse-chorus cycles, a formal strategy with no parallel in the human corpus.} 

The normalized musical form trigram profiles per genre (Figure~\ref{fig:musical_form}) reveal a consistent pattern across styles. Both Suno and Udio show a reduced range of formal options relative to Lastfm, with intro-verse-chorus sequences markedly under-represented and solo sections largely absent. This is consistent with how both platforms operate: users generate a song segment that begins directly with a verse-chorus cycle and can later be extended via the web interface. The result is that intro sections, which precede the core structure, and solo sections, which typically appear later in human-composed music, are rarely generated in the initial output. \highLight[r3!20!white]{The same genre-ontology caveat raised for Figure~\ref{fig:trigrams_per_style} applies here: the Jazz polygon in Figure~\ref{fig:musical_form} should not be read as a direct cross-collection comparison of jazz formal conventions, since the tracks grouped under that label differ substantially in character across the three corpora.}

A key confound is song duration. Suno tracks average 175 seconds (approximately 2.9 minutes) and Udio tracks average 143 seconds (approximately 2.4 minutes), compared to 261 seconds (approximately 4.4 minutes) for Lastfm (Figure~\ref{fig:durations}). Shorter tracks have fewer opportunities for structural elaboration (bridges, instrumental breaks, key changes), so the reduced diversity observed in Suno and Udio may partly reflect a generation-length constraint rather than a purely compositional bias. \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{Udio's duration distribution is notably bimodal: a sharp spike below one minute reflects tracks published at the platform's default clip length of 32 seconds without extension, while the secondary peak near 130 seconds corresponds to tracks generated at the platform's longest available clip length (2 minutes and 10 seconds).}

A further caveat concerns formal labels and musical function. The All-In-One model assigns labels based on acoustic properties, but a verse-chorus alternation in a generated track may represent static loop repetition rather than the narrative-arc form those labels imply in conventional songwriting~\citep{nieto2020audio}. Distinguishing conventional pop form from non-teleological loop structures would require additional features such as melodic contour, rhythmic analysis, or lyric structure.

\subsection{Outcome Homogenization and Creative Agency}
\label{sec:homogenization}
The harmonic compression documented in RQ2 and the structural narrowing documented in RQ3 both point toward a form of outcome homogenization, consistent with the concept of algorithmic monoculture, where shared components across AI systems increase the likelihood of convergent outputs~\citep{bommasani2022picking}. Suno exemplifies this pattern, converging on a statistical peak of canonical pop progressions (Figure~\ref{fig:pop_prevalence}, Table~\ref{tab:suno_enrichment}), also visible in the Byte-Pair Encoding of the most common p-trigrams (Figure~\ref{fig:bpe_trigrams_suno}). The output does not reflect the variety of harmonic practice or formal organization found in human-composed music.
Several mechanisms may contribute to this convergence. First, training data bias amplification, in which generative models learn to reproduce only the most frequent patterns in their training corpora. Since contemporary pop music dominates streaming platforms and likely constitutes a significant portion of Suno's training data, these harmonic patterns are overrepresented in its outputs. Second, optimization toward perceptual plausibility encourages models to generate music that sounds ``familiar'' to broad audiences, reinforcing existing harmonic conventions rather than exploring innovative progressions. Third, the lack of harmonic-prompting capabilities in current platforms means that users cannot directly specify harmonic content, instead relying on the models' learned biases toward common progressions. Fourth, Suno's developers may intentionally be targeting users who prefer a narrower spectrum of music, focusing on commercial modern pop styles.
Our data cannot determine which of these factors is primary, but the contrast with Udio is informative. Udio produces more diverse harmonic and structural outputs than would be expected from a presumably comparable training distribution, suggesting that model architecture and generation strategy play a role beyond the training data alone.
These platforms promise to make music creation widely accessible, but generating convincing audio recordings does not necessarily entail preserving musical knowledge or variety. \highLight[r3!20!white]{We note that a restricted harmonic vocabulary is not in itself a compositional deficiency: twelve-bar blues, punk, and minimal techno are all built on narrow chord sets by deliberate stylistic choice. Our claim is different: the data show that Suno applies a single harmonic template across all stated genres, a platform-level convergence that is independent of user intent. This is consistent with Suno's own positioning: as documented by \citet{neely_suno}, the platform's leadership has described it explicitly as gamifying musical creation.} This raises questions about whether these tools genuinely support creative agency among musicians with any degree of musical knowledge~\citep{wiggins2007compositional, wiggins2016musical, lin2023promptsexploringdesignspace, tan2024we}. The prevalence of simple, repetitive formal structures (as revealed by our BPE analysis shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bpe_trigrams_suno}) further suggests that current platforms may be optimized to generate familiar, commercially viable content rather than to support experimentation or skill acquisition. This optimization strategy may serve commercial interests, but it limits the creative potential of AI as a compositional tool.

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our analysis of 60,014 tracks reveals systematic differences between AI-generated and human-composed popular music. Suno converges on a narrow harmonic and formal space: canonical pop progressions are overrepresented at 2.5 to 4.6 times the rate found in human-composed music, and its structural repertoire is dominated by simple verse-chorus cycles. Udio's harmonic and structural distributions are statistically closer to those of human-composed music across all measures examined. These findings, and their implications for outcome homogenization and creative agency, are discussed in detail in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
One direction for future work follows directly from the limitations identified in Section~\ref{sec:disc_mir}: improving functional harmonic labeling to handle modulations, modal mixture, and key ambiguity would strengthen corpus-scale analyses that currently depend on a single global key estimate. \highLight[niceBlue!20!white]{More broadly, this work demonstrates that fully automated, open-source MIR pipelines can now support corpus-scale harmonic and formal analysis at a scale and vocabulary richness that were not previously practical. The combination of rich-vocabulary chord estimation and semantic section labeling, applied here to 60,014 tracks, offers a replicable methodology for computational musicology and MIR as AI-generated music continues to grow in both volume and diversity.}

\section{Ethics and consent}

Although the full ramifications of AI-generated material remain uncertain, the sheer speed and volume at which these systems can create new works demand scholarly attention \citep{Sturm2024a,pelly2025mood}. Because our study centers on music, we gathered examples from the commercial services {\em Suno} and {\em Udio}. Doing so raises a set of ethical issues. Even when a site's terms of use do not appear to permit automated collection, the legitimacy of repurposing that material for research---particularly under a "fair use" rationale---deserves scrutiny. As a publicly funded team investigating AI music and its broader consequences, we rely on the text-and-data-mining exception laid out in the EU Copyright Directive 2019/790 \citep{EUDirective2019} to justify our data acquisition.
